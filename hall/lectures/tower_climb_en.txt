========================================
 IRON CLIMB // CONSTRUCTION YARD // 1981
 narrated by VINNIE, silk-screen press
========================================
> Sit down. Wipe your hands. The
> screens are still wet.

> My name is Vinnie. I run a silk-
> screen press on the third floor of
> a brick building in the coin-op
> district of Chicago. From 1974 to
> 1995 I printed every arcade flyer
> you ever saw in a bowling alley
> lobby. Atari, Bally Midway, Williams,
> Stern, Centuri — they all came to
> me. Six colours on glossy
> cardstock, 11 by 17, double-sided.
> Two days turnaround if you were
> nice to me.

> Let me tell you about a cabinet
> that walked into my shop in late
> 1981. I cannot say its name. Its
> name is owned by a company in
> Kyoto and the company is very
> protective. So I will tell you
> only what happened.

> ── PART 1: THE ERA BEFORE ──

> Before this cabinet, platform
> games — if you can call them that
> — were not journeys. They were
> situations. One screen. A stack
> of horizontal beams. Ladders
> joining them. A small worker at
> the bottom. Trouble at the top.

> The vocabulary was old. Climb
> the ladder. Jump the obstacle.
> Reach the next floor. Get to the
> top.

> What was missing was a REASON
> to climb. Why is the worker
> climbing? What is trouble at the
> top, exactly? Who is throwing
> the obstacles?

> Nobody had answered those
> questions on a single screen.
> The genre was waiting.

> ── PART 2: THE CABINET ARRIVES ──

> November 1981. A young man in a
> wrinkled shirt walks into my
> shop. He has a portfolio under
> his arm. He speaks careful
> English with a Kyoto accent.

> He says: "Vinnie-san, we are
> bringing two thousand cabinets to
> the United States. Our parent
> company has invested. They are
> very nervous. The cabinets ship
> in three weeks. We need flyers."

> He opens the portfolio. Inside is
> hand-painted artwork. A
> construction yard. Steel girders
> in a zig-zag, four floors
> stacked. A small man at the
> bottom, in a red cap and blue
> overalls. (I'm not allowed to
> say more about him.) A large
> creature at the top, throwing
> things down. (Allowed even less
> to say about that one.) A woman
> at the top, tied up.

> I look at it. I say: "Where is
> she? Who is he? What is the
> creature?" He says: "The story
> is on the back. Print it on the
> back."

> ── PART 3: THE STORY ──

> The story was: a carpenter was
> at a construction site. The
> creature stole his fiancée. The
> creature climbed to the top of
> the unfinished building, taking
> her with it. The carpenter
> chased. The creature, from the
> top, threw barrels and fireballs
> down the structure to stop him.

> That was IT. A villain. A
> hostage. A motive. Climbing as
> NARRATIVE.

> "Why a carpenter?" I asked. The
> young man said: "He is not a
> carpenter actually. He is a
> plumber later." This made no
> sense to me at the time. I let
> it go.

> "Why barrels?" I asked. He
> said: "We considered guns. The
> arcade boards in the US do not
> want guns this year. So,
> barrels. Heavy. Round. Will
> roll. Cannot accidentally hit
> a child."

> "Why the ladders?" I asked. He
> said: "Vertical movement that is
> not jumping. Two kinds of
> motion. Players need both."

> ── PART 4: THE FLYERS ──

> I printed five thousand flyers.
> Six colours. Glossy cardstock.
> The hand-painted artwork was
> beautiful. I delivered them on
> a Tuesday. The young man came
> back the next Monday, looking
> tired and happy. He said: "We
> are short. We need ten thousand
> more."

> Eight months later he came back
> for sixty thousand more. The
> cabinets had become — how do I
> say this — the cabinets had
> become the entire industry.
> Bowling alleys, pizza parlours,
> hotel lobbies, laundromats —
> everywhere — the cabinet was
> there. The cabinet was earning
> $400 a week per location. Some
> arcades had four of them in a
> row.

> By the end of 1982 the cabinet
> had earned its parent company
> 280 million dollars. The parent
> company stopped being nervous.
> The parent company started
> building a HOME console.

> The home console — you know how
> this story ends. The grey box.
> The cartridge. The carpenter
> who was actually a plumber. The
> entire industry, possibly the
> entire popular culture of the
> next forty years, balanced on
> the shoulders of a small man
> in a red cap who first appeared
> climbing girders in a Kyoto
> sketchbook on its way to my
> printing press.

> ── PART 5: WHAT WE BUILT ──

> Below is IRON CLIMB. Our
> construction-yard homage. A
> nervous technician at the
> bottom. A broken crane at the
> top. Rolling barrels coming
> down. Ladders between girders.
> An oldest-in-games heroic
> instruction:

> GET TO THE TOP.

> No famous ape. No famous
> carpenter. No famous damsel.
> A nervous technician, a broken
> crane, and the panic of timing
> a ladder while a barrel is
> coming.

> ── PART 6: WHY IT STILL WORKS ──

> Because vertical is harder than
> horizontal. Because climbing
> takes longer than running.
> Because falling means re-
> climbing. Because the top is
> always visible — you can see
> exactly how far you have to
> go and exactly how many
> obstacles you have to dodge,
> and you do not get to forget
> any of it.

> Because the single screen,
> framed by the cabinet bezel,
> was a small stage. You watched
> a tiny play. You were the
> actor. The other actor was the
> creature at the top. The
> stakes were always rescue or
> failure. There was no third
> option.

> A small play. Three minutes.
> Two thousand performances per
> evening, per cabinet, across
> thousands of cabinets, for
> several years. By 1985 the
> entire popular culture of the
> West knew, without having
> ever been told, that you climb
> ladders, jump barrels, get to
> the top.

> ── PART 7: MAMMA MIA ──

> I retired in 1995. The silk
> press is in a museum now —
> the Computer History Museum in
> Mountain View, donated. The
> young man from Kyoto became,
> eventually, the most famous
> game designer in the world.
> The carpenter became the most
> famous video-game character in
> history. The grey-box company
> became one of the three
> largest game companies on
> Earth.

> And the original artwork — the
> hand-painted construction yard
> with the zig-zag girders and
> the small red-capped man — was
> donated to the same museum. I
> still go visit, occasionally.
> It is behind glass now.

> Climb, amico. Jump the
> barrels. Reach the top. That
> is the whole story. It was
> the whole story in 1981 and
> it is the whole story now.

> Vinnie out. Press the FIRE
> button to jump.

> GET TO THE TOP.

> Controls:
> D-pad left/right — run along girders.
> D-pad up/down — climb a ladder.
> FIRE — jump.

> The original 1981 arcade cabinet and
> its characters belong to their
> respective rights-holders. IRON CLIMB
> is an original homage to the
> vertical platformer shape: girders,
> ladders, timing and panic.
